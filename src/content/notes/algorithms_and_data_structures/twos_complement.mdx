<div class="px">
<header class="hero">
<div class="kicker">Binary arithmetic — how machines subtract by adding</div>
<p class="lede">A computer's adder only knows how to add. Subtraction, negative numbers, and the sign bit itself all have to be built out of that one operation — and the trick that makes it possible is the <strong>complement</strong>: for every number, a partner that completes it to a round total. Flip every bit and you get the one's complement; flip and add one and you get the two's complement, and with it <math><mi>A</mi><mo>−</mo><mi>B</mi></math> becomes <math><mi>A</mi><mo>+</mo><mo>(</mo><mo>−</mo><mi>B</mi><mo>)</mo></math>, an addition. This page builds the idea from the definition up: what a complement is with respect to a number, the three historical ways to write signed values, negation, addition, subtraction by addition, the end-around carry that one's complement arithmetic needs, signed overflow, and the exact range each system buys you.</p>
<div class="keybox"><p><strong>The one-sentence idea.</strong> The complement of <math><mi>x</mi></math> with respect to a fixed total <math><mi>T</mi></math> is what <math><mi>x</mi></math> needs added to it to reach <math><mi>T</mi></math>: it is <math><mi>T</mi><mo>−</mo><mi>x</mi></math>. Choose <math><mi>T</mi><mo>=</mo><msup><mn>2</mn><mi>n</mi></msup></math> in <math><mi>n</mi></math> bits and that partner <em>is</em> the negation — so subtraction collapses into addition, and one adder circuit does everything.</p></div>
</header>
</div>

## 1 — What a complement is, with respect to a number

<div class="px">
<p>"Complement" is a relative word: a number never has <em>a</em> complement in the abstract, only a complement <strong>with respect to</strong> some fixed reference total. The complement of <math><mi>x</mi></math> with respect to <math><mi>T</mi></math> is the amount that must be added to <math><mi>x</mi></math> to reach exactly <math><mi>T</mi></math>, equivalently, <math><mi>T</mi><mo>−</mo><mi>x</mi></math>. Two gears meshing, two angles making a right angle, two quantities making a round sum: in every case the complement is defined by the total it completes.</p>
<p>The reference totals that matter are the roundest ones in each base. In two-digit decimal the candidates are <math><mi>T</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>99</mn></math> (all nines, one less than the modulus) and <math><mi>T</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>100</mn></math> (the modulus itself, <math><msup><mn>10</mn><mn>2</mn></msup></math>). They give the <strong>9's complement</strong> and the <strong>10's complement</strong>. The 9's version has a gift: since <math><mn>99</mn><mo>−</mo><mi>x</mi></math> subtracts each digit of <math><mi>x</mi></math> from 9 independently, it needs no borrows — each digit is simply flipped to its partner under 9 (0↔9, 1↔8, 2↔7, 3↔6, 4↔5). In binary the same two choices reappear, and the digit-flip gift becomes a bit-flip.</p>

## 2 — Sign-magnitude vs one's vs two's complement

<div class="px">
<p>An <math><mi>n</mi></math>-bit register holds <math><msup><mn>2</mn><mi>n</mi></msup></math> patterns. Unsigned arithmetic spends them all on <math><mn>0</mn></math> to <math><msup><mn>2</mn><mi>n</mi></msup><mo>−</mo><mn>1</mn></math>. To also write negative numbers, three systems compete — all three agree on the positives (sign bit 0, plain binary magnitude) and differ only in how a pattern with sign bit 1 is read.</p>
<table class="formulas">
<tr><th>Sign-magnitude</th><td>The top bit is a pure sign flag; the remaining <math><mi>n</mi><mo>−</mo><mn>1</mn></math> bits hold <math><mo>|</mo><mi>x</mi><mo>|</mo></math>. Reading is effortless — this is how humans write <math><mo>−</mo><mn>5</mn></math>, but the adder must compare signs and magnitudes before it can even start, and <math><mn>0</mn></math> gets two patterns: <code>0000</code> and <code>1000</code> (<math><mo>+</mo><mn>0</mn></math> and <math><mo>−</mo><mn>0</mn></math>).</td></tr>
<tr><th>One's complement</th><td>A negative is the bitwise flip of its positive: pattern value <math><mo>=</mo><mo>−</mo><mover><mi>x</mi><mo>~</mo></mover></math> read as "flip to negate". Negation is free and symmetric, but <math><mn>0</mn></math> still has two patterns (<code>0000</code> and <code>1111</code>), and addition needs the end-around carry correction of Step 6.</td></tr>
<tr><th>Two's complement</th><td>A negative is the flip plus one: pattern <math><mi>p</mi></math> with top bit 1 has value <math><mi>p</mi><mo>−</mo><msup><mn>2</mn><mi>n</mi></msup></math>. Equivalently, read the bits as a weighted sum where the top bit weighs <math><mo>−</mo><msup><mn>2</mn><mrow><mi>n</mi><mo>−</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow></msup></math> instead of <math><mo>+</mo><msup><mn>2</mn><mrow><mi>n</mi><mo>−</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow></msup></math>. One pattern for zero, one plain unsigned adder for everything — and the spend is asymmetric: one extra negative, <math><mo>−</mo><msup><mn>2</mn><mrow><mi>n</mi><mo>−</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow></msup></math>, with no positive partner.</td></tr>
</table>

## 3 — Negation: each system flips something different

<div class="px">
<p>Negation is where the three systems show their personalities most plainly. Sign-magnitude flips one bit — the sign — and touches nothing else. One's complement flips every bit: negation <em>is</em> the complement operation, which is exactly the diminished-radix complement of Step 1. Two's complement flips every bit and adds one, completing the move from the diminished total <math><msup><mn>2</mn><mi>n</mi></msup><mo>−</mo><mn>1</mn></math> to the full modulus <math><msup><mn>2</mn><mi>n</mi></msup></math>.</p>

## 4 — Addition: one unsigned adder serves every sign

<div class="px">
<p>Here is the payoff of the two's complement definition. Because a negative pattern is its value modulo <math><msup><mn>2</mn><mi>n</mi></msup></math>, adding two patterns bit by bit — carries and all, with any carry out of the top bit simply discarded — produces the pattern of the true sum, whenever that sum fits the range. No sign comparison, no magnitude subtraction, no cases: the same circuit that adds unsigned numbers adds signed ones, and the result is even correct read as unsigned when both inputs are. (One's complement almost manages this: its additions are correct up to the end-around carry repair of Step 6. Sign-magnitude addition, by contrast, is a small decision tree of its own.)</p>

## 6 — End-around carry: the carry that comes back

<div class="px">
<p>Why does one's complement addition need a repair that two's complement addition doesn't? The diminished modulus is the reason. Adding two one's complement patterns works modulo <math><msup><mn>2</mn><mi>n</mi></msup><mo>−</mo><mn>1</mn></math>, not <math><msup><mn>2</mn><mi>n</mi></msup></math>, but the register discards multiples of <math><msup><mn>2</mn><mi>n</mi></msup></math>. Each carry out of the top bit therefore throws away <math><msup><mn>2</mn><mi>n</mi></msup></math> when it should only have thrown away <math><msup><mn>2</mn><mi>n</mi></msup><mo>−</mo><mn>1</mn></math>: the sum comes out exactly <strong>1 too small</strong>. The fix is to take that carry and add it back in at the least significant bit — the carry <em>ends around</em>. Two's complement, whose modulus is the register's own <math><msup><mn>2</mn><mi>n</mi></msup></math>, discards exactly the right amount and needs no repair. Step through all four behaviours:</p>

## 7 — Overflow: the sign bit lies

<div class="px">
<p>Discarding the carry-out is safe only while the true result stays inside <math><mo>[</mo><mo>−</mo><msup><mn>2</mn><mrow><mi>n</mi><mo>−</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow></msup><mo>,</mo><mspace width="0.3em"/><msup><mn>2</mn><mrow><mi>n</mi><mo>−</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow></msup><mo>−</mo><mn>1</mn><mo>]</mo></math>. Step outside it and the pattern still forms — addition never fails — but it now encodes a value of the wrong sign: <math><mn>100</mn><mo>+</mo><mn>50</mn></math> in 8 bits produces <code>10010110</code>, which reads as <math><mo>−</mo><mn>106</mn></math>. That event is <strong>signed overflow</strong>, and it is not the carry-out: <math><mn>100</mn><mo>+</mo><mo>(</mo><mo>−</mo><mn>50</mn><mo>)</mo></math> also produces a carry-out of 1, yet its answer 50 is exactly right. Overflow has a precise test, in two equivalent forms:</p>
<table class="formulas">
<tr><th>Sign rule</th><td>Overflow happens exactly when the two addends have the <em>same</em> sign and the result's sign differs from theirs. Adding numbers of opposite signs can never overflow: the true sum lies between them, hence inside the range. The same rule covers subtraction, reading <math><mi>A</mi><mo>−</mo><mi>B</mi></math> as <math><mi>A</mi><mo>+</mo><mo>(</mo><mo>−</mo><mi>B</mi><mo>)</mo></math>: overflow needs <math><mi>A</mi></math> and <math><mi>B</mi></math> of opposite signs and a result whose sign differs from <math><mi>A</mi></math>'s.</td></tr>
<tr><th>Carry rule (what hardware tests)</th><td>Let <math><msub><mi>c</mi><mtext>in</mtext></msub></math> be the carry <em>into</em> the sign bit and <math><msub><mi>c</mi><mtext>out</mtext></msub></math> the carry <em>out of</em> it. Then<span class="eqwrap"><math display="block"><mi>V</mi><mo>=</mo><msub><mi>c</mi><mtext>in</mtext></msub><mo>⊕</mo><msub><mi>c</mi><mtext>out</mtext></msub></math></span>Overflow (<math><mi>V</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>1</mn></math>) exactly when the two carries differ — the sign column's arithmetic disagrees with its neighbours about which side of the boundary the sum fell on. This XOR is the overflow flag in every mainstream CPU.</td></tr>
</table>
