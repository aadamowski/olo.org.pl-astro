<p class="mn-eyebrow">Special relativity · Minkowski geometry</p>
<p class="mn-lede">A moving clock ticks slowly — that part depends only on its *speed*. So where does acceleration come in? It comes in by **bending the clock's path through spacetime**. Once you draw motion the way Minkowski taught us to draw it, acceleration stops being a footnote to time dilation and becomes the thing that decides *which* history a clock lives through — and therefore how much time it actually experiences.</p>

<div class="mn-keybox">
<p><strong>The one-sentence version:</strong> time dilation at any instant is set by velocity alone, <em>γ</em> = 1/√(1 − <em>v</em>²/<em>c</em>²). Acceleration never appears in that formula — but acceleration is what <em>changes</em> <em>v</em>, curves the worldline, and makes the total elapsed proper time depend on the whole journey. That is why the accelerating twin comes home younger.</p>
</div>

## 1 · The Minkowski map

<p class="mn-stepnum">Step 1 of 9</p>
A Minkowski diagram throws away two space dimensions and promotes time to an axis. Horizontal position is *x*; vertical position is *ct* — time multiplied by the speed of light, so time and space share the same units (here, light-years). Any object's history becomes a curve on this map: its **worldline**. A dot is an *event* — a place at a moment.

Three reading rules make every later diagram fall into place:

<div class="mn-readbox">
<h3>How to read it</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Slope is speed.</strong> A vertical worldline is an object sitting still. The more a worldline leans away from vertical, the faster the object moves — lean is literally <em>v</em>/<em>c</em>.</li>
<li><strong>The cone is a speed limit drawn as geometry.</strong> A worldline must always stay <em>inside</em> the future light cone of every event it passes through. Leaning to 45° would mean moving at <em>c</em>.</li>
<li><strong>Clocks measure their own worldline.</strong> The time a traveller experiences — their <em>proper time</em> τ — is a property of the curve they trace, not of the map's vertical axis. The next step makes that precise.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<p>Keep one contrast in mind: on an ordinary road map, the straight line between two towns is the <em>shortest</em> route. In Minkowski's geometry the sign in front of the time term flips that intuition — and the flip is the whole story of time dilation.</p>

## 2 · Proper time is length

<p class="mn-stepnum">Step 2 of 9</p>
For a short segment of worldline, the interval formula gives the proper time ticked by a clock riding that segment: *c*²d<em>τ</em>² = *c*²d<em>t</em>² − d<em>x</em>². Time contributes positively, space *subtracts*. So a clock that also moves through space accumulates *less* proper time than one that goes straight up in time. The calibration curves below — hyperbolas of constant τ — are the Minkowski equivalent of the circles a compass draws on an ordinary map: every point on one curve is the same proper time from the origin event.

<div class="mn-readbox" style="margin-top:0">
<h3>How to read it</h3>
<ul>
<li>Each hyperbola marks events reachable with the <strong>same proper time</strong> τ from departure — τ = 1, 2, 3 years.</li>
<li>Moving sideways pushes you <em>along</em> the hyperbola, not outward: at 0.6<em>c</em>, reaching the τ = 2 curve takes 2.5 years of Earth time but still only 2 years on your clock.</li>
<li>These hyperbolas, not circles, are spacetime's "distance contours" — a direct picture of the minus sign in the interval.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div class="mn-callout"><p><strong>The reversed triangle inequality.</strong> Between two events, the straight (unaccelerated) worldline has the <em>longest</em> proper time of any route. Any detour through space — any leaning, bending worldline — totals <em>less</em> τ. "The shortest-looking path on paper is the longest in time." Steps 4–6 are all consequences of this one fact.</p></div>

## 3 · Velocity dilates

<p class="mn-stepnum">Step 3 of 9</p>
Differentiate the interval and the proper-time rate falls out immediately: d<em>τ</em> = d<em>t</em> √(1 − *v*²/*c*²) = d<em>t</em>/*γ*. A clock moving at speed *v* through your frame ticks γ times slower — not because anything is wrong with it, but because part of its motion through spacetime is spent going sideways. On the diagram, the moving observer's axes themselves tilt: their time axis leans by the angle whose tangent is *v*/*c*, and their space axis leans by the *same* angle — so their "now" slices the diagram diagonally. That symmetric tilt is the Lorentz transformation, as a picture.

## 4 · Acceleration bends

<p class="mn-stepnum">Step 4 of 9</p>
Velocity is the *slope* of a worldline. Acceleration is its *rate of change of slope* — the worldline **bends**. An unaccelerated clock draws a straight line and (Step 2) collects the maximum possible proper time between two events. An accelerated clock curves away and collects less. At each instant, the curved worldline is tangent to some straight worldline: the traveller momentarily *is* an inertial observer with that velocity, and that instant's tilted frame from Figure 3. Acceleration is the continuous hand-off from one tilted frame to the next.

<h3>The precise statement</h3>
<ul>
<li>A clock's instantaneous rate depends only on its instantaneous <em>v</em> — this is the <strong>clock hypothesis</strong>, tested to accelerations of ~10<sup>18</sup> m/s² (Step 9).</li>
<li>But its <em>total</em> elapsed time is the accumulation along the whole curve — and acceleration is the only way to change the curve.</li>
<li>In the traveller's own terms, what they feel is <strong>proper acceleration</strong> α = γ³<em>a</em> (for motion along the velocity): the reading on an accelerometer they carry. It is absolute — no frame choice makes a bent worldline straight.</li>
</ul>

<div class="mn-myth"><p><strong>Common misconception, corrected:</strong> "Acceleration causes time dilation, the way gravity does." In special relativity there is no extra acceleration term to add. Two clocks with the same <em>v</em>(<em>t</em>) history tick identically even if one reached that history gently and the other violently. Acceleration matters <em>indirectly but decisively</em>: it authors the velocity history — and at a turnaround, it breaks the symmetry between two observers, as Step 6 shows.</p></div>

## 5 · Adding up a journey

<p class="mn-stepnum">Step 5 of 9</p>
Chop any worldline — however bent — into tiny straight pieces. Each piece is short enough that the velocity barely changes across it, so Step 3's formula applies piece by piece: Δ<em>τ</em> ≈ Δ<em>t</em> √(1 − *v*²/*c*²). Add the pieces and let them shrink, and you get the master formula of this page. Acceleration is inside it implicitly: *v*(*t*) is whatever function the accelerations produced.

<div class="mn-formula">
<em>τ</em> = ∫ √(1 − <em>v</em>(<em>t</em>)²/<em>c</em>²) d<em>t</em> &nbsp;=&nbsp; ∫ d<em>t</em> / <em>γ</em>(<em>t</em>)
<small>Proper time along any worldline. This single integral is the "Minkowski spacetime formula" for time dilation with acceleration included: feed it a velocity history — any history, however it was accelerated into being — and it returns exactly what the travelling clock reads.</small>
</div>

Worked miniature — a clock whose speed profile over 4 Earth seconds is: accelerate 0 → 0.6<em>c</em> in the first second, cruise 2 seconds, decelerate back to rest in the last second. Applying the integral in three chunks:

## 6 · The twin paradox

<p class="mn-stepnum">Step 6 of 9</p>
Each twin, coasting, sees the other's clock run slow — so who is younger at reunion? The standoff is resolved by the worldlines. The stay-at-home twin follows *one* straight worldline: a single inertial frame the whole time. The traveller follows *two* different straight worldlines joined at the turnaround — and joining them requires acceleration. At the turnaround the traveller's "now" line (Figure 3) swings violently: in the traveller's accounting, a whole slab of Earth history jumps from "future" to "past" during that brief manoeuvre. That swing is where Earth's clock "gains" the years. No swing, no reunion discrepancy; acceleration is what makes the two histories genuinely different rather than two descriptions of one history.

<h3>The same trip, three ways of seeing it</h3>
<ul>

<p>Note what the integral (Step 5) says about the turnaround itself: making it gentler or sharper barely changes the total — the 2-year deficit is earned during the long fast legs. Step 7 looks at the one case where acceleration is sustained the whole trip.</p>

## 7 · Constant acceleration

<p class="mn-stepnum">Step 7 of 9</p>
Hold the accelerometer pinned at a constant proper acceleration *g* forever. In Earth coordinates the velocity asymptotes to *c* — *v*/*c* = tanh(*gτ*/*c*) — and the worldline is not a parabola but a **hyperbola**: (*x* + *c*²/*g*)² − (*ct*)² = (*c*²/*g*)². Its asymptotes are light rays: behind the rocket there is a **Rindler horizon** at distance *c*²/*g* — about 0.97 light-years for 1<em>g</em> — beyond which light signals can never catch up, however long they chase.

<p>Meanwhile γ = cosh(<em>gτ</em>/<em>c</em>) grows exponentially in the traveller's own time: after 1 year at 1<em>g</em> the rocket does 0.77<em>c</em> (γ ≈ 1.58); after 2 years, 0.97<em>c</em> (γ ≈ 3.99); after 5 years γ ≈ 86. Earth time <em>t</em> = (<em>c</em>/<em>g</em>) sinh(<em>gτ</em>/<em>c</em>) races ahead of ship time τ ever faster — sustained acceleration is the most powerful time-dilation machine special relativity allows, which is why the "1<em>g</em> rocket" is a staple of interstellar thought experiments.</p>

## 8 · Quasi-3D: cones &amp; spirals

## 9 · What acceleration does not do

<p class="mn-stepnum">Step 9 of 9</p>
<div class="mn-cards">
<div class="mn-card"><h3>Acceleration does…</h3><p><strong>Change the velocity, hence γ, moment by moment</strong> — it steers the worldline's slope. <strong>Break symmetries:</strong> whoever accelerates is unambiguously the one who changed frames. <strong>Decide the total:</strong> through <em>v</em>(<em>t</em>), it fixes the value of the proper-time integral, so different acceleration programmes between the same events yield different elapsed times.</p></div>
<div class="mn-card"><h3>Acceleration does not…</h3><p><strong>Add its own term to the rate.</strong> There is no <em>a</em> in d<em>τ</em> = d<em>t</em>/γ. <strong>Need to be large to matter:</strong> a feather-touch turnaround still breaks the twin symmetry. <strong>Act like gravity in SR</strong> — the resemblance (the equivalence principle) is real but is general relativity's story; in flat Minkowski spacetime, acceleration alone produces no extra "gravitational" dilation beyond the velocity integral.</p></div>
</div>

<div class="mn-callout"><p><strong>The bridge to gravity.</strong> Einstein's equivalence principle says a uniformly accelerating rocket's cabin is locally indistinguishable from standing in gravity — and in the rocket, the velocity-integral already makes the nose clock and tail clock tick at different steady rates (they have different velocity histories in any single inertial frame… and the cabin's own Rindler coordinates make the rate difference look exactly like gravitational redshift, Δ<em>τ</em>/<em>τ</em> ≈ <em>gh</em>/<em>c</em>²). Acceleration in flat spacetime is thus the training ground where gravitational time dilation is first visible — but the Minkowski formula above remains the complete SR account.</p></div>

<h3 id="four-lines">The whole story in four lines</h3>
<div class="mn-formula" style="text-align:left;white-space:normal;line-height:1.9">
1 · Interval: *c*²d<em>τ</em>² = *c*²d<em>t</em>² − d<em>x</em>² — space motion is subtracted from time.<br />
2 · Rate: d<em>τ</em> = d<em>t</em>/γ(*v*) — velocity alone sets the instantaneous tick rate.<br />
3 · Total: *τ* = ∫ d<em>t</em>/γ(*v*(*t*)) — and acceleration writes the *v*(*t*) inside.<br />
4 · Constant *g*: *v*/*c* = tanh(*gτ*/*c*), worldline a hyperbola, horizon at *c*²/*g*.
</div>

<p>So: does acceleration cause time dilation? Not directly — no term, no extra effect, at any instant. But remove acceleration from the universe and no clock could ever change its velocity, turn around, orbit, or come home — every worldline would be straight, every twin the same age, and the integral would have nothing interesting to integrate. Acceleration is the author of every time-dilation story; velocity is just its handwriting.</p>

function artanh(x){return 0.5*Math.log((1+x)/(1-x));}
function updateLab(){
  var L=parseFloat(dS.value), beta=parseFloat(bS.value), g=parseFloat(lgS.value);
  var a=1.03*g; /* per year, c=1 */

  }
  tauA=artanh(beta)/a; tA=gam*beta/a;
  cruiseD=L-2*xa;

function drawLab(L,tTrip,peakBeta,gamPeak,a,triangular){
  var path=$("labPath"), star=$("labStar");
  if(!path) return;
  var xs=Math.min(105,470/L), ys=310/tTrip;
  var ox=90, oy=395;
  function X(x){return ox+xs*x;} function Y(t){return oy-ys*t;}
  star.setAttribute("x1",X(L)); star.setAttribute("x2",X(L));
  /* Proxima Centauri reference at 4.2465 ly: positioned through the same X() scale
     as everything else, so when a larger destination distance rescales the plot
     (xs drops below its 105 cap), the marker slides inward with the scale. Its
     label flips side near the right edge so it never runs off the plot. */
  var proxLine=$("labProximaLine"), proxDot=$("labProximaDot"), proxLab=$("labProximaLab"), proxSub=$("labProximaSub");
  if(proxLine){
    var pxP=X(4.2465);
    proxLine.setAttribute("x1",fmt(pxP,1)); proxLine.setAttribute("x2",fmt(pxP,1));
    if(proxDot){ proxDot.setAttribute("cx",fmt(pxP,1)); }
    if(proxLab){
      var labelRight=pxP<=400, lxP=labelRight?pxP+11:pxP-11, anchorP=labelRight?"start":"end";
      proxLab.setAttribute("x",fmt(lxP,1)); proxLab.setAttribute("text-anchor",anchorP);
      if(proxSub){ proxSub.setAttribute("x",fmt(lxP,1)); proxSub.setAttribute("text-anchor",anchorP); }
    }
  }
  var tauA=artanh(peakBeta)/a, tA=gamPeak*peakBeta/a, xa=(gamPeak-1)/a;
  var cruiseD=triangular?0:(L-2*xa), tCruise=triangular?0:cruiseD/peakBeta;
  var tArr=2*tA+tCruise;
  var pts=[[0,0]], i, tau, tp;
  /* Acceleration burn: hyperbolic motion sampled by proper time. Many fine samples
     (not 8): at high cruise speed / low g the cosh/sinh terms change fastest near
     the end of the burn, and coarse chords cut the corner and distort the shape. */
  var NSEG=40;
  for(i=1;i<=NSEG;i++){ tau=tauA*i/NSEG; pts.push([(Math.cosh(a*tau)-1)/a, Math.sinh(a*tau)/a]); }
  /* cruise */
  if(!triangular){ pts.push([xa+cruiseD, tA+tCruise]); }
  /* deceleration burn: acceleration profile mirrored about the arrival event */
  for(i=1;i<=NSEG;i++){ tp=tauA*(1-i/NSEG); pts.push([L-(Math.cosh(a*tp)-1)/a, tArr-Math.sinh(a*tp)/a]); }
  pts.push([L,tArr]);
  /* return leg: one-way worldline mirrored in time */
  var all=pts.slice();
  for(i=pts.length-2;i>=0;i--){ all.push([pts[i][0], 2*tArr - pts[i][1]]); }
  var d=all.map(function(p,j){ return (j===0?"M":"L")+fmt(X(p[0]),1)+" "+fmt(Y(p[1]),1); }).join(" ");
  path.setAttribute("d",d);
}
