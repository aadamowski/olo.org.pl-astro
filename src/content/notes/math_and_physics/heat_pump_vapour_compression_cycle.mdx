<div class="hp">
<p class="kicker">Thermodynamics · Vapour-compression cycle</p>

<p class="lede">A heat pump does not <em>make</em> heat the way a resistance heater or a furnace does. It spends a small amount of work <strong>W</strong> (in kilowatts, kW) to transport a larger amount of heat <strong>Q</strong> (also in kW) from a cold place to a warm place. Reverse the plumbing with one valve and the same machine becomes an air conditioner. This page follows the refrigerant around the loop in both directions, puts real numbers — temperatures in °C and kelvin (K), pressures in bar, enthalpies in kJ/kg — on every step, and then looks at the part most diagrams leave out: <strong>hysteresis</strong>, the reason the real machine never quite retraces its own path.</p>

<div class="callout">
<p><strong>The one-line energy balance.</strong> Everything in this explainer is bookkeeping on a single statement of the first law of thermodynamics for one full cycle:</p>
<p style="font-size:19px;text-align:center"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" display="block"><msub><mi>Q</mi><mi>H</mi></msub><mo>=</mo><msub><mi>Q</mi><mi>C</mi></msub><mo>+</mo><mi>W</mi></math></p>
<p>Heat delivered at the hot side (Q<sub>H</sub>) equals heat absorbed at the cold side (Q<sub>C</sub>) plus the work you paid for (W). If W = 1.0&nbsp;kW of electricity moves Q<sub>C</sub> = 2.4&nbsp;kW of outdoor heat indoors, you receive Q<sub>H</sub> = 3.4&nbsp;kW of heating. Nothing is created — the outdoor air is left slightly colder.</p>
</div>
</div>

## 1 · The four-part cycle

<div class="hp">
<p>Four components, one sealed loop of refrigerant (commonly R32 or R410A in residential units, increasingly R290/propane in newer European models):</p>
<ol class="steps">
<li><strong>Evaporator</strong> — cold, low-pressure liquid refrigerant absorbs heat and boils. Boiling at low pressure happens at low temperature, which is why the coil can pull heat out of air at −10&nbsp;°C.</li>
<li><strong>Compressor</strong> — an electric motor (typically 0.5–3&nbsp;kW in a home unit) squeezes the vapour. Squeezing raises both its pressure (roughly 3–4×) and its temperature — to 60–90&nbsp;°C at the discharge.</li>
<li><strong>Condenser</strong> — the hot, high-pressure vapour gives up its heat and condenses back to liquid.</li>
<li><strong>Expansion valve</strong> — the liquid is throttled through a narrow orifice. Pressure and temperature crash, and a cold liquid–vapour mixture re-enters the evaporator. This step is deliberately irreversible — remember it when we reach hysteresis.</li>
</ol>
<p>A <strong>reversing valve</strong> (a sliding four-way valve in the refrigerant line) swaps which coil plays which role. The compressor, valve and physics do not change — only the direction of heat flow does. That is the whole difference between "heat pump" and "air conditioner".</p>
</div>

## 3 · Efficiency done properly: COP and the Carnot limit

<div class="hp">
<p>"Efficiency" above 100% is not a violation of anything, because a heat pump is not converting work into heat — it is <em>moving</em> heat. The correct figure of merit is the <strong>coefficient of performance</strong>, and its theoretical ceiling comes from the reversible (Carnot) refrigerator run between the same two reservoirs. The derivation below keeps every step visible before any special case is taken.</p>

<table class="formulas">
<tr><th>Step 1 — First law, full cycle</th><td><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><msub><mi>Q</mi><mi>H</mi></msub><mo>=</mo><msub><mi>Q</mi><mi>C</mi></msub><mo>+</mo><mi>W</mi></math> &nbsp; Energy in = energy out over one complete cycle (the refrigerant returns to its starting state, so its net energy change is zero). ## 4 · Hysteresis: why the machine never retraces its path

<div class="hp">
<p><em>Hysteresis</em> means the state of a system depends on its history, not just on its current inputs: the path out and the path back are different, and the loop between them encloses an area that measures energy dissipated or behaviour delayed. Heat pumps exhibit it in three distinct places — and confusing them is common, so they are separated here.</p>

<h3 id="hp-hyst-a">(a) Control hysteresis — the thermostat deadband</h3>
<p>If a thermostat switched the compressor on at 20.99&nbsp;°C and off at 21.01&nbsp;°C, the compressor would start and stop every few seconds ("short-cycling"), destroying efficiency and the compressor itself — most wear happens at start-up, and a cycle needs minutes to reach steady pressures. So controllers deliberately build in a <strong>deadband</strong>: switch <em>on</em> only when the room falls to (<math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><mtext>setpoint</mtext><mo>−</mo><mfrac><mtext>deadband</mtext><mn>2</mn></mfrac></math>), switch <em>off</em> only when it rises to (<math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML"><mtext>setpoint</mtext><mo>+</mo><mfrac><mtext>deadband</mtext><mn>2</mn></mfrac></math>). Between the thresholds, the machine keeps doing whatever it was already doing — that history-dependence <em>is</em> hysteresis. The price is a temperature swing; the reward is long, efficient cycles. Manufacturers typically use 0.5–1.0&nbsp;°C; inverter units soften the effect by modulating speed instead of switching fully off.</p>

<h3 id="hp-hyst-b">(b) Cycle hysteresis — irreversibility fattens the loop</h3>
<p>On the pressure–enthalpy diagram (Figure 5), a reversible Carnot machine would trace a rectangle and return along its own path. The real loop does not close on itself reversibly: throttling (3→4) throws away the work a turbine could have recovered, heat only crosses the coils because of finite temperature gaps (≈ 7–12 K), and the compressor's friction turns extra work into discharge heat. Each irreversibility widens the gap between the real loop and the reversible one — the thermodynamic sense in which the cycle has "memory" of having been driven. Defrost is the most visible episode: on humid days near 0&nbsp;°C, frost chokes the outdoor coil, so the machine briefly <em>reverses into cooling mode</em>, melting the frost with indoor heat, then flips back. Whether it is "in defrost" at, say, −1&nbsp;°C depends on how long it has been running and how much frost has accumulated — history again, not just temperature.</p>

<h3 id="hp-hyst-c">(c) What hysteresis is not</h3>
<p>The reversing valve's few-percent cooling penalty, and the capacity drop at low temperature, are ordinary losses, not hysteresis — they are the same on the way up and on the way down. Reserve the word for genuine path-dependence: deadbands, defrost state, and the irreversible widening of the cycle loop.</p>

function simulate(dead,tout2){
  var set=21, T=set-dead/2-0.01, on=true, dt=0.1; // minutes per step (6 s)
  var lossK=0.055/26; // per min per K
  var pts=[], states=[];
  var onTime=0, starts=0, total=180;
  var prevOn=false;
  for(var t=0;t<=total;t+=dt){
    var loss=lossK*(T-tout2)*dt*10; // dt in min: loss rate per min = 0.055*(T-tout)/26
    loss=0.055*(T-tout2)/26*dt;
    var gain= on? 0.16*dt:0;
    T+= gain - loss*1.0 - (on?0:0) ;
    // net when on already includes? gain is gross heating 0.215, net ~0.16 at default; simpler: heating adds 0.215
    // (kept transparent in text as net +0.16 before losses approx)
    if(on && T>=set+dead/2){ on=false; }
    if(!on && T<=set-dead/2){ on=true; }
    if(on&&!prevOn) starts++;
    prevOn=on;
    if(on) onTime+=dt;
    pts.push([t,T]); states.push(on?1:0);
  }
  return {pts:pts,states:states,starts:starts,onTime:onTime,total:total};
}
