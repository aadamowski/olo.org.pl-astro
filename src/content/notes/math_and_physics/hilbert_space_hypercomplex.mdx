<p class="hs-kicker">Interactive explainer — Inner products, completeness, and the Cayley–Dickson ladder</p>
<p class="hs-lede">Two great constructions run through modern mathematics. One goes <em>up in dimension without end</em>: Hilbert space, where vectors can be whole functions and sums can have infinitely many terms. The other doubles dimension in discrete jumps — ℝ → ℂ → ℍ → 𝕆 → 𝕊 → the 32-dimensional trigintaduonions — and pays a toll at every jump, surrendering one cherished law of arithmetic each time. This page builds both, step by step, and shows exactly where they meet.</p>

## 1 — Hilbert space: geometry with infinitely many directions

<p>A <strong>Hilbert space</strong> is a vector space equipped with an <strong>inner product</strong> — a machine that takes two vectors and returns a number measuring how much they point the same way — and one extra demand: the space must be <strong>complete</strong>, meaning every sequence of vectors that <em>ought</em> to converge to something actually converges to a vector inside the space. Inner product gives geometry (lengths, angles, projections); completeness guarantees the geometry has no holes.</p>

<p>Everything starts with the inner product. In the plane, for <math><mi>u</mi><mo>=</mo><mo>(</mo><msub><mi>u</mi><mn>1</mn></msub><mo>,</mo><msub><mi>u</mi><mn>2</mn></msub><mo>)</mo></math> and <math><mi>v</mi><mo>=</mo><mo>(</mo><msub><mi>v</mi><mn>1</mn></msub><mo>,</mo><msub><mi>v</mi><mn>2</mn></msub><mo>)</mo></math>, it is the familiar dot product. The definition, and the three quantities it buys you, are collected in one table:</p>

### Completeness: no holes in the space

<p>Geometry alone does not make a Hilbert space. Consider the rational numbers ℚ on a line: you can add them and measure distances, yet the sequence 1, 1.4, 1.41, 1.414, … — rationals closing in on <math><msqrt><mn>2</mn></msqrt></math> — is a <strong>Cauchy sequence</strong> (its terms get arbitrarily close to <em>each other</em>) whose limit is missing from ℚ. A space with such a hole is an <em>inner product space</em> but not a Hilbert space. Completeness is the demand that every Cauchy sequence converges to a point of the space itself.</p>

The explorer below builds a square wave out of sine waves, one basis direction at a time.</p>

## 3 — The Cayley–Dickson construction: how each level is built from the last

<p>The doubling machine is due to Cayley and Dickson. Its input is an algebra A with a <strong>conjugation</strong> (an operation <math><mi>a</mi><mo>↦</mo><msup><mi>a</mi><mo>*</mo></msup></math> that flips the sign of every imaginary part, like complex conjugation). Its output is a new algebra <math><msup><mi>A</mi><mn>2</mn></msup></math> whose elements are <em>ordered pairs</em> (a, b) of elements of A — so the dimension exactly doubles. Three definitions do all the work. They are stated once, in full generality, and then every algebra in the tower is just this recipe applied repeatedly:</p>

## 4 — Walking the tower, one algebra at a time

### ℝ — the real numbers (dimension 1)

<p>The ground floor is a Hilbert space already: ℝ is <math><msup><mi>ℝ</mi><mn>1</mn></msup></math> with inner product <math><mo>⟨</mo><mi>a</mi><mo>,</mo><mi>b</mi><mo>⟩</mo><mo>=</mo><mi>a</mi><mi>b</mi></math>. It is the only level with a total <strong>ordering</strong> compatible with arithmetic (<math><mi>a</mi><mo>&lt;</mo><mi>b</mi></math> implies <math><mi>a</mi><mo>+</mo><mi>c</mi><mo>&lt;</mo><mi>b</mi><mo>+</mo><mi>c</mi></math>, and products of positives are positive). Everything above ℝ¹ will have to give that up — there is no way to say whether i is "positive" that survives multiplication, since <math><msup><mi>i</mi><mn>2</mn></msup><mo>=</mo><mo>−</mo><mn>1</mn></math> would then have to be positive too.</p>

### ℂ — the complex numbers (dimension 2): pay ordering, gain roots

<p><math><mi>ℂ</mi><mo>=</mo><msup><mi>ℝ</mi><mn>2</mn></msup></math> as a Hilbert space, with the extra gift of multiplication. The imaginary unit <math><mi>i</mi><mo>=</mo><mo>(</mo><mn>0</mn><mo>,</mo><mn>1</mn><mo>)</mo></math> is the first Cayley–Dickson ℓ. Multiplying by i [rotates the plane by 90° counter-clockwise](/notes/math_and_physics/i_complex_euler_formula/#part-2--the-complex-plane-and-its-four-major-directions), which is why <math><msup><mi>i</mi><mn>2</mn></msup><mo>=</mo><mo>−</mo><mn>1</mn></math> is forced geometrically: two quarter-turns make a half-turn, and a half-turn sends every number to its negative.</p>

### ℍ — the quaternions (dimension 4): pay commutativity, gain 3D rotation

<p>Hamilton's quaternions (1843) write <math><mi>q</mi><mo>=</mo><mi>a</mi><mo>+</mo><mi>b</mi><mi>i</mi><mo>+</mo><mi>c</mi><mi>j</mi><mo>+</mo><mi>d</mi><mi>k</mi></math> with three imaginary units obeying the relations he carved into Brougham Bridge: <math><msup><mi>i</mi><mn>2</mn></msup><mo>=</mo><msup><mi>j</mi><mn>2</mn></msup><mo>=</mo><msup><mi>k</mi><mn>2</mn></msup><mo>=</mo><mi>i</mi><mi>j</mi><mi>k</mi><mo>=</mo><mo>−</mo><mn>1</mn></math>. From those, the full multiplication of units follows — and it is <em>ordered</em>: <math><mi>i</mi><mi>j</mi><mo>=</mo><mi>k</mi></math> but <math><mi>j</mi><mi>i</mi><mo>=</mo><mo>−</mo><mi>k</mi></math>. Rotations in 3D do not commute either (tip a book forward-then-yaw versus yaw-then-forward), and that is precisely why quaternions, not despite but because of non-commutativity, are the working language of 3D rotation in graphics, robotics and spacecraft attitude control.</p>

### 𝕆 — the octonions (dimension 8): pay associativity, keep division

<p>An octonion has seven imaginary units <math><msub><mi>e</mi><mn>1</mn></msub><mo>…</mo><msub><mi>e</mi><mn>7</mn></msub></math> (with <math><msub><mi>e</mi><mn>0</mn></msub><mo>=</mo><mn>1</mn></math>), each squaring to <math><mo>−</mo><mn>1</mn></math>. Their multiplication is encoded by the <strong>Fano plane</strong>: seven points, seven lines of three points each. The rule: the three units on any line form a quaternion-style triple — the product of any two of them is the third, with the sign fixed by the line's cyclic direction (click a line in Figure 10). ### 𝕊 — the sedenions (dimension 16): pay division itself

### 𝕋 — the trigintaduonions (dimension 32): the construction outlives the arithmetic

<p>Nothing stops the machine. One more doubling gives the <strong>trigintaduonions</strong>: 32-dimensional numbers <math><mi>x</mi><mo>=</mo><mo>∑</mo><msub><mi>x</mi><mn>i</mn></msub><msub><mi>e</mi><mn>i</mn></msub></math> with <math><msub><mi>e</mi><mn>0</mn></msub><mo>=</mo><mn>1</mn></math> and thirty-one imaginary units, built as pairs of sedenions, <math><mo>(</mo><mi>a</mi><mo>,</mo><mi>b</mi><mo>)</mo></math> with <math><mi>a</mi><mo>,</mo><mi>b</mi><mo>∈</mo><mi>𝕊</mi></math>, multiplied by the same one-line rule. ## 5 — Where the two constructions meet

<p>The tower and Hilbert space were never separate stories. Equip any level of the tower with its standard inner product — for <math><mi>x</mi><mo>=</mo><mo>∑</mo><msub><mi>x</mi><mn>i</mn></msub><msub><mi>e</mi><mn>i</mn></msub></math> and <math><mi>y</mi><mo>=</mo><mo>∑</mo><msub><mi>y</mi><mn>i</mn></msub><msub><mi>e</mi><mn>i</mn></msub></math>, take <math><mo>⟨</mo><mi>x</mi><mo>,</mo><mi>y</mi><mo>⟩</mo><mo>=</mo><mo>∑</mo><msub><mi>x</mi><mn>i</mn></msub><msub><mi>y</mi><mn>i</mn></msub></math>, equivalently <math><mo>⟨</mo><mi>x</mi><mo>,</mo><mi>y</mi><mo>⟩</mo><mo>=</mo><mi>Re</mi><mo>(</mo><mi>x</mi><mo>·</mo><msup><mi>y</mi><mo>*</mo></msup><mo>)</mo></math> — and:</p>

<div class="hs-note">
<b class="hs-tag">The one-paragraph summary</b>
<p style="margin:0">Start with ℝ. Ask for more directions while keeping arithmetic perfect, and Cayley–Dickson doubling gives you ℂ, ℍ, 𝕆 — charging ordering, then commutativity, then associativity — after which Hurwitz's theorem proves the shop is closed: 𝕊 and the trigintaduonions beyond it keep the construction but lose division to zero divisors. Ask instead for more directions while keeping only <em>geometry</em> — inner product, length, angle, projection — plus the completeness that makes limits land, and you get Hilbert space, which happily extends to infinitely many dimensions, houses Fourier series and quantum states, and contains every floor of the tower as a finite-dimensional special case.</p>
