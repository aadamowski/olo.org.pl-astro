<div class="lat">
<div class="kicker">Post-quantum cryptography — the geometry underneath</div>

<p class="lede" style="margin-top:16px">A lattice is an infinite, perfectly regular grid of points — but described by a <strong>basis</strong> that can make the same grid look either beautifully simple or hopelessly tangled. That gap between a good basis and a bad basis is where modern post-quantum cryptography lives: easy if you hold the good basis, apparently intractable if you only see the bad one.</p>

<div class="keybox">
<p><strong>The one-sentence idea.</strong> Publish a skewed, long-vector basis for a lattice; keep a short, nearly orthogonal basis secret. Anyone can scramble a message into the lattice, but only the holder of the good basis can reliably find the nearest lattice point and unscramble it — and no known algorithm, classical or quantum, closes that gap in high dimensions.</p>
</div>
</div>

## 1 — What is a lattice?

<div class="lat">
<p>Take two vectors that are not on the same line. Every integer combination of them is a lattice point:</p>

<tr><th>Lattice generated by a basis</th><td><math display="block"><mrow><mi>L</mi><mo>=</mo><mo>\{</mo><mi>a</mi><msub><mi mathvariant="bold">b</mi><mn>1</mn></msub><mo>+</mo><mi>c</mi><msub><mi mathvariant="bold">b</mi><mn>2</mn></msub><mo>:</mo><mi>a</mi><mo>,</mo><mi>c</mi><mo>∈</mo><mi>ℤ</mi><mo>\}</mo></mrow></math></td></tr>
<tr><th>Fundamental parallelogram area</th><td><math display="block"><mrow><mtext>area</mtext><mo>=</mo><mo>|</mo><mi>det</mi><mo>(</mo><mi>B</mi><mo>)</mo><mo>|</mo></mrow></math><span style="font-size:.9rem;color:var(--math-ink2)">One lattice point per cell of this area, on average — the density of the lattice.</span></td></tr>
</table>

<p>The lattice itself is just the set of points. The <em>basis</em> is a choice of generating vectors — and, crucially, the same lattice has infinitely many bases. Some are short and nearly perpendicular. Others are long, nearly parallel, and hide the lattice's simple structure. Everything below turns on that distinction.</p>
</div>

## 4 — The shortest vector problem (SVP)

<div class="lat">
<p>Every lattice with determinant 1 in 2D is guaranteed a surprisingly short non-zero vector — Minkowski's theorem promises one of length at most <math><mrow><msqrt><mn>2</mn></msqrt><mo>≈</mo><mn>1.41</mn></mrow></math>. Finding it, however, is another matter when your only basis is bad. In our example the shortest vector is (1, 0), of length 1. In the good basis it is simply the first basis vector. In the bad basis it is hidden as a difference:</p>

<div class="lat">
<p>Given a point that is <em>not</em> on the lattice, find the nearest lattice point. <p><strong>Why dimension is the security dial.</strong> In 2D, Gauss reduction finds the good basis instantly. In a few hundred dimensions, the best reduction algorithms (BKZ with large block sizes, sieving) need astronomically many operations — and unlike factoring or discrete logarithms, no quantum algorithm is known that changes this picture. ## 8 — The essential problems and the real schemes

<div class="lat">

<div class="readbox">
<h3>The whole story in four lines</h3>
<ul>
<li>A lattice is a regular grid; a basis is a way of describing it, and descriptions vary from excellent to terrible.</li>
<li>Rounding and nearest-point search are easy with a good basis and fail with a bad one — you measured the failure yourself in Figure 4.</li>
<li>LWE turns that geometric gap into algebra: exact equations plus small errors, decodable only via a closest-vector search.</li>
<li>At dimensions of 512 to 1024, no known classical or quantum algorithm bridges the gap in feasible time — which is why these schemes are the post-quantum standards.</li>
</ul>
