<p class="ce-lede">The number line only lets you go forwards and backwards. The complex plane adds a second direction — up and down — and *i* is simply the name of “one step in that new direction.” Once you see multiplication by *i* as a quarter-turn, everything else follows: i² = −1 stops being a mysterious rule, and Euler’s formula e<sup>iθ</sup> = cos θ + i sin θ turns out to be compound growth that has learned to turn.</p>

## Part 1 — Why does i² have to be −1?

Start with the honest problem. On the ordinary number line, squaring any number — positive or negative — gives something positive or zero:

<div class="ce-formula">3<sup>2</sup> = 9 &nbsp;·&nbsp; (−3)<sup>2</sup> = 9 &nbsp;·&nbsp; 0<sup>2</sup> = 0 &nbsp;·&nbsp; x<sup>2</sup> ≥ 0, always</div>

So no real number squares to −1. Equations like x² + 1 = 0 simply have no answer on the line. Mathematicians had two choices: declare such equations unsolvable forever, or **invent a new direction** and see whether the resulting system stays consistent. They chose the second — and consistency is exactly what forces i² = −1. There are three ways to see why, and they all agree.

### ① By definition — it’s the job description

*i* is *defined* as a number whose square is −1, written i = √−1. That isn’t circular trickery; it’s how every number system grew. “−3” was once just as scandalous (“you can’t have fewer than nothing”), and “2/3” required inventing fractions. Each time, we kept the old rules of arithmetic and asked: what new object would make the unsolvable solvable? For x² = −1, the answer is an object with exactly one defining property: **square it and you get −1**.

### ② By geometry — two quarter-turns must face you backwards

Here is the deeper reason the definition *works*. Multiplying by −1 flips the number line 180°: 3 becomes −3. Now suppose multiplying by *i* means rotating 90° counter-clockwise (the diagrams in Part 2 justify this). Then multiplying by *i* twice is a 180° rotation — which is exactly what multiplying by −1 does. Two operations that do the same thing to *every* number must be the same operation:

<div class="ce-formula">i · i = i² &nbsp;=&nbsp; rotate 90° then 90° &nbsp;=&nbsp; rotate 180° &nbsp;=&nbsp; ×(−1)</div>

So i² = −1 isn’t an arbitrary decree. *Any* consistent “quarter-turn number” is forced to square to −1.

<div class="ce-callout">
<h3 id="by-consistency">③ By consistency — the alternatives break arithmetic</h3>
<p>Suppose instead we declared i² = +1. Then i would just be ±1 in disguise — nothing new. Suppose i² = 0. Then (1 + i)(1 − i) = 1 − i² = 1, but also expanding carefully gives contradictions with the distributive law once you try to divide. Only i² = −1 keeps addition, multiplication, and division all working — and it pays off immediately: *every* polynomial equation now has solutions. That payoff is the real test, and i passes it.</p>
</div>

A complex number is then just a pair of instructions — **go this far along the real axis, then this far up the imaginary axis**:

<div class="ce-formula big">z = a + b·i &nbsp;&nbsp;<span style="font-size:.8em">a = real part, b = imaginary part</span></div>

The word “imaginary” is unfortunate historical baggage (Descartes meant it as an insult). The imaginary component is no less real than the “negative” component of a bank balance: it measures displacement along a genuine, usable direction — the vertical one.

## Part 2 — The complex plane and its four major directions

Draw the real numbers horizontally and the imaginary numbers vertically. Every complex number a + bi is a point — equivalently, an arrow from the origin. The four unit arrows, pointing right, up, left, and down, are the compass points of this plane. Multiplying any number by one of them rotates it; multiplying by *i* in particular rotates it exactly one compass point counter-clockwise.

## Part 3 — The familiar story: compound growth and e

Put $1 in an account paying 100% interest per year. If the interest is credited once, you end with $2. Credited twice (50% every six months), you end with (1 + ½)² = $2.25 — a little more, because the first interest itself earns interest. Credited n times a year, each credit adds 1/n of your current balance:

<div class="ce-formula big">( 1 + 1/n )<sup>n</sup></div>

As n grows the total creeps upward but never explodes — it settles at a specific number, e ≈ 2.71828. This limit *defines* e, and its continuous version is the most important growth formula in mathematics:

<div class="ce-formula big">e<sup>x</sup> = lim<sub>n→∞</sub> ( 1 + x/n )<sup>n</sup></div>

## Part 4 — Now add the imaginary component

Make one tiny change to the compounding factor. Instead of growing by a real 1/n each step, grow by an *imaginary* 1/n — i/n:

<div class="ce-formula big">( 1 + i/n )<sup>n</sup> &nbsp;→&nbsp; e<sup>i</sup> &nbsp; as n → ∞</div>

Each step now multiplies by a number that is *mostly* 1 (so the size barely changes) but tilted slightly upward (so the direction rotates a little). Multiplication in the plane does two things at once — it **scales** by the factor’s length and **rotates** by the factor’s angle. The factor (1 + i/n) has length √(1 + 1/n²), just above 1, and angle arctan(1/n), a small turn. Repeat it n times and the small turns add up to exactly 1 radian (about 57.3°) while the extra length washes out to 1.

**How to read each diagram below:** every figure shows the two versions of the same formula side by side, for the same n and the same step counts. On the **left**, the raw compound-growth line chart from Part 3 — (1 + 1/n)<sup>k</sup> climbing along toward e. On the **right**, the same formula with the imaginary component added — (1 + i/n)<sup>k</sup> plotted in the complex plane, with the orange dots on the axis marking where the plain real steps land, so the two can be compared directly. Watch the teal path bend further as n increases.

## Part 5 — The limit: Euler’s formula

Nothing in Part 4 depended on the total turn being 1 radian. Replace i by i·θ — tilt each step so the turns add up to θ radians instead — and the same limit gives the general statement:

<div class="ce-formula big">e<sup>iθ</sup> = cos θ + i sin θ</div>

Read it geometrically: e<sup>iθ</sup> is the point you reach by taking infinitely many infinitesimal “grow-and-turn” steps whose turns total θ. Because the growth part washes out to a factor of exactly 1, you end up *on the unit circle*, at angle θ — whose coordinates are, by definition, (cos θ, sin θ). Exponential growth and circular motion are the same process, viewed along different axes.

<h3 id="euler-identity">The famous special case</h3>
<p>Set θ = π — a half-turn, travelled by infinitely many tiny turns. Euler’s formula gives e<sup>iπ</sup> = cos π + i sin π = −1 + 0i, i.e.</p>
<div class="ce-formula" style="text-align:center">e<sup>iπ</sup> + 1 = 0</div>
<p>Euler’s identity. It is not a coincidence bolted together from five constants; it is the statement “a half-turn lands you at −1,” written in exponential language — the same fact as i² = −1, one level up.</p>
</div>

### Why this matters beyond beauty

- **Rotation becomes multiplication.** To rotate any signal, vector, or waveform by θ, multiply by e<sup>iθ</sup> — one operation instead of a matrix.
- **Waves become exponentials.** cos θ and sin θ are just the real and imaginary parts of e<sup>iθ</sup>, so oscillations can be added, differentiated, and filtered with ordinary algebra. This is the working language of AC circuits, audio, radio, and quantum mechanics.
- **Growth and turning unify.** e<sup>x</sup> grows; e<sup>ix</sup> turns; e<sup>(a+bi)x</sup> spirals — one formula covers bank accounts, pendulums, and decaying oscillations.

<div class="ce-callout">
<p><strong>The one-sentence summary:</strong> i is a quarter-turn; i² = −1 because two quarter-turns make a half-turn; and Euler’s formula is what compound growth looks like when every growth step is also a tiny turn.</p>
</div>

   single-digit values (n = 1..9), then ramps up geometrically to 576 = 9·2⁶
   (n doubles for every 1/6 of the remaining track). */
function nFromSlider(rw){
  rw=Math.max(0,Math.min(1000,rw));
  if(rw<=250){ var q=(rw/250)*8; return Math.max(1,Math.min(9,1+Math.floor(q))); }
  var t=(rw-250)/750;
  return Math.min(576, Math.round(9*Math.pow(2, 6*t)));
}
