<div class="px">
<header class="hero">
<div class="kicker">Public-key cryptography — the geometry underneath</div>
<p class="lede">Take a smooth cubic curve. Its points can be <strong>added</strong> with nothing more than a ruler: draw a line, find where it meets the curve again, reflect. That one geometric rule, repeated, builds a group where multiplication is easy but undoing it — finding <em>how many times</em> a point was added to itself — is computationally hopeless. Every TLS handshake, Bitcoin signature, and passkey login rests on that asymmetry.</p>
<div class="keybox"><p><strong>The one-sentence idea.</strong> A private key is just a number d. The public key is the point Q = dG, computed by adding a fixed generator point G to itself d times. Going forward takes microseconds even for a 256-bit d; going backward, recovering d from Q, is the elliptic curve discrete logarithm problem, and no known algorithm does it in feasible time.</p></div>
</header>
</div>

## 1 — The curve: a cubic with a symmetry

<div class="px">
<p>An elliptic curve over the real numbers is the set of points satisfying a short Weierstrass equation, plus one extra point at infinity that will act as the identity. Two things matter about the shape: it is symmetric about the x-axis (if (x, y) is on the curve, so is (x, −y)), and it must be <em>smooth</em> — no cusps or self-crossings — which is exactly what the discriminant condition below guarantees.</p>

## 2 — Point addition: the chord-and-tangent rule

<div class="px">
<p>Here is the whole group law in one picture. A line meets a cubic in at most three points. So: draw the line through P and Q; it must hit the curve at a third point, call it R′; reflect R′ across the x-axis and define that to be P + Q. Reflection is what makes the operation associative and gives every point an inverse — the reflected point is −P, and P + (−P) is the vertical-line case, defined to be 𝒪.</p>

<h3>How to read this</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Closure:</strong> the sum of two curve points is always another curve point — the reflection step is what guarantees it.</li>
<li><strong>Inverses:</strong> reflect P to get −P. The line through them is vertical, never meets the curve a third time in the plane, and the sum is defined as 𝒪.</li>

## 3 — Scalar multiplication: the one-way street

<div class="px">
<p>Now move the same equation to a finite field, where real cryptography lives. Work modulo the prime p = 17 on the curve below. The "curve" is now just 18 scattered points plus 𝒪, a group of order n = 19, which is prime, so every non-identity point generates the whole group. Fix the generator G = (5, 1). Scalar multiplication means adding G to itself k times, and the efficient way is <em>double-and-add</em>: read k in binary, double the running point for each bit, and add G whenever a bit is 1. A 256-bit private key needs only about 256 doublings and at most 256 additions — never 2<sup>256</sup> additions.</p>

## 4 — Key exchange: ECDH

<div class="px">
<p>Diffie–Hellman needs an operation that commutes: a(bG) must equal b(aG). Scalar multiplication does exactly that, because both are just (ab)G. Alice picks a secret a and publishes A = aG; Bob picks a secret b and publishes B = bG. Alice computes aB, Bob computes bA, and both hold the same point — while an eavesdropper who sees G, A, and B cannot combine them without solving a discrete log.</p>

## 5 — Signatures: ECDSA

<div class="px">
<p>A signature proves "the holder of d signed this message" without revealing d. The signer commits to a one-time secret k by publishing the x-coordinate of kG, then mixes that commitment with the message hash and the private key. Verification recombines the public key Q = dG with the signature in a way that reconstructs the commitment point — but only if the signature really was built from d.</p>

## 6 — Why the discrete log is hard

<div class="px">
<p>Everything above is public except one problem: given G and Q = dG, find d. On the toy curve an attacker can simply walk the multiples of G until the target appears — try it below; on average the target turns up halfway through the group. The devastating fact is how that walk scales. The best known generic attack, Pollard's rho method, needs about the <em>square root</em> of the group order — for a 256-bit curve that is still roughly 2<sup>128</sup> point operations, and unlike factoring there is no subexponential index-calculus attack: elliptic curves offer no smooth structure for it to exploit. Quantum computers are the exception — Shor's algorithm solves this problem in polynomial time, which is why post-quantum schemes exist — but classically, the gap below is the whole story.</p>

<p>Put the 128-bit row in time units: 2<sup>128</sup> is about 3.4 × 10<sup>38</sup>. At a trillion point operations per second — far beyond any single machine — the search runs about 3.4 × 10<sup>26</sup> seconds, roughly 1.1 × 10<sup>19</sup> years, against a universe about 1.4 × 10<sup>10</sup> years old. That is why a 256-bit elliptic curve key, small enough to fit in a sentence, protects traffic that a 3072-bit RSA key is needed to match.</p>
<div class="callout"><p><strong>The honest caveats.</strong> The hardness is a well-tested belief, not a proof — no one has proven the discrete logarithm is hard on any curve. It holds only for carefully chosen curves (large prime-order group, no special structure), correct implementations, and secret scalars that stay secret. And a large fault-tolerant quantum computer running Shor's algorithm would end it — the one attack that does not care how big the group is.</p></div>
